KoD / Ongoing research program

Design Ecosystems

Understanding how design decisions connect all people, built environments, cultural communities, natural systems, and human institutions from the first brief through generational use is essential. Why?

Because decisions about a site, a brief, materials, funding, and access affect far more than the finished project. They influence who participates, whose expertise is recognized, how a place or product is used, and what future generations will need to maintain. KoD studies these relationships to examine responsibility from the first decision through long-term care.

Research and analysis by Brandie Janow

Research model

The layers of a design ecosystem

Design depends on more than the name credited on a project. Clients, researchers, designers, makers, workers, communities, and those responsible for upkeep all contribute to what happens. So do land, materials, culture, education, and institutional decisions. This six-part model examines how these contributions connect, who has influence, and who takes responsibility across the life of a project.

Example · Public building

Why examine the connections?

Consider a library being planned for a neighborhood. The brief, site, community needs, construction materials, specialist skills, funding, and maintenance plans are all related. An early decision about access, materials, or repair can matter long after the building opens.

Looking at the whole ecosystem lets us ask who participated in those choices, whose knowledge was used, and what the building will need across generations.

Research questions

How these relationships work

At the center of the inquiry are the people whose knowledge, labor, decisions and lived experience influence design. The research questions address authorship, influence, participation and the responsibilities that continue throughout a project's life.

Who directs the work?

How are questions framed, briefs developed and decisions negotiated among commissioners, practitioners and communities?

Who contributes knowledge?

How do research, technical skill, making, lived experience and local understanding inform the process?

How is expertise recognized?

How are contributions identified, credited, and understood across collaborative projects?

Where do consequences travel?

How do choices affect workers, users, communities, materials, habitats and long-term outcomes?

Primary field documentation

Learning from design practice

Field documentation captured at the Design Museum in London in May 2025 contributed observations on the relationships between designers, makers and users, the visibility of production expertise, ethical manufacturing and the lifecycle of materials.

People and authorship

Design involves many contributors.

Exhibition material foregrounded the role of makers, collaborators, producers and users alongside the people credited with directing a project.

Production and materiality

Making involves specialist knowledge.

Fabrication records, textile processes and material displays revealed the decisions, skills and technical relationships involved in bringing work into use.

Public knowledge

Institutions preserve the process.

Research residencies, collection interpretation and educational programs showed how design knowledge is shared, questioned and carried forward.

Primary visual field documentation: Design Museum, London, May 2025. Observations have been organized within the ongoing KoD research archive.

Life-centric research

Design across its whole life

The layered model brings material origins, labor, use, maintenance and ecological effects into one research perspective. It creates a basis for examining responsibility across time and across the people and living systems connected to a design.

Continuing inquiry

Where the research is developing

The next phase brings the layered model into project documentation and comparative studies. It will examine how responsibilities are distributed, how communities influence outcomes, and how contributions are represented in records of design work.

Map participation

Compare contributors and decision pathways across different types of design practice.

Trace authorship

Examine project credits, production records and the roles through which expertise becomes visible.

Follow influence

Study how research, engagement and feedback affect briefs and subsequent decisions.

Study consequences

Connect material lifecycles, work, use and ecological effects within selected cases.
