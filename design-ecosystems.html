Design Ecosystems

Design decisions affect people, cultures, the conditions under which things are produced, and the natural environment.

A visual identity influences how a community is represented. Clothing and products depend on materials, factories, and skilled labor. An interior or building affects construction crews, nearby residents, water, and wildlife habitats. This research examines those connections across the design industry, including whose decisions carry the greatest consequences and whether the people responsible for production have fair pay, safe conditions, and basic rights.

Lead Researcher and Author: Brandie JanowArchitectural advisor: Nathan Saint Clare

Research model

The layers of a design ecosystem

Education, public policy, client requirements, cultural history, skilled labor, and natural resources all affect what designers can do. The six areas below help identify what needs attention and who is involved, from the earliest research through making, use, and upkeep.

Research questions

Questions requiring evidence

The six areas identify the people, resources, and decisions involved in design. They cannot tell us whether workers have been treated fairly or whether public concerns have changed a proposal. Those questions need records, interviews, and careful comparisons. They remain open until the evidence has been examined.

Who sets pay and conditions?

How do client contracts, suppliers, and subcontractors affect wages, working hours, and safety for people who construct, manufacture, or install a design?

Can workers report abuse?

Who checks labor conditions, and what protection exists for people who report unsafe conditions, withheld pay, or exploitation?

Does consultation change decisions?

When residents or local groups raise concerns, can we trace those comments to changes in drawings, budgets, materials, or the final brief?

Who bears the later costs?

Who pays for repairs and maintenance, and how are environmental effects and consequences for nearby communities assessed over time?

Why KoD began

Life centered design

One reason for establishing Knowledge of Design was a concern about the limits of human centered design. Designers are often asked to focus on the needs of a customer or the people who will use a building, product, or service. But the effects do not stop with those people.

Making a product can require water, energy, and raw materials. Building on a site can affect soil, trees, and animal habitats. Those consequences belong in the research and decisions, alongside human needs. Life centered design asks us to consider the natural world from the beginning.

A 2018 scientific study of Earth's biomass estimated that humans account for approximately 0.01% of all biomass measured by carbon mass. This is a measure of mass, not a count of species. It helps put the ecological impact of human activity into perspective.

Biodesign also offers ways to work directly with biology, including research involving fungi, algae, and bacteria. Other work examines biodiversity, materials, habitats, and what happens to products after they are no longer needed. Read more about biodesign at UC Davis.
