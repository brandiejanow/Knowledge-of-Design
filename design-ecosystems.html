Design Ecosystems

Every field of design is connected to the people, places, cultures, materials, and natural environments around it.

A visual identity can change how a community is represented. An interior affects daily life, access, and the materials used in a building. A new development can alter streets, water, and wildlife habitats. This research considers those connections across graphic design, fashion, products, interiors, services, architecture, and the wider design industry.

Lead Researcher and Author: Brandie JanowArchitectural advisor: Nathan Saint Clare

Research model

The layers of a design ecosystem

Education, public policy, client requirements, cultural history, skilled labor, and natural resources all affect what designers can do. The six areas below help identify what needs attention and who is involved, from the earliest research through making, use, and upkeep.

People, culture, and place

Understanding where the work belongs

Places have their own languages, customs, histories, climate, and ways of living. Designers need to understand these conditions, especially when working in a community or country they do not know. This matters in a logo, a chair, a public service, or a major building.

Different fields

What changes with the work?

A graphic designer needs to understand how symbols and words are read locally. An interior designer needs to consider how people move through a space, who cleans and repairs it, and where materials come from. An architect working abroad must account for local building methods, climate, public life, and nearby habitats.

These are different responsibilities. Each requires research before decisions are fixed.

Who should be involved?

Have the people affected by the work had a chance to explain their needs, concerns, and everyday experiences?

What should we learn about the place?

Which customs, languages, local skills, histories, and environmental conditions could change the decisions being made?

What expertise is needed?

When should designers involve researchers, craftspeople, engineers, local businesses, or people with first hand experience?

What could the work change?

How might the result affect access, working conditions, maintenance, cultural life, water, plants, or animal habitats?

Field observations

How design is made and recorded

During a visit to the Design Museum in London in May 2025, exhibitions showed the roles of makers, manufacturers, designers, and others who contribute to finished work. Displays of materials and production methods offered useful examples for this study.

People behind the work

Who made it?

Exhibition accounts identified makers, collaborators, and producers whose work contributed to the finished pieces.

Materials and methods

How was it made?

Textiles, fabrication records, and material samples showed the skills and choices involved in production.

Recording and teaching

How can others learn from it?

Exhibition labels and educational materials offered examples of how decisions and methods can be explained to the public.

Why KoD began

Life centered design

One reason for establishing Knowledge of Design was a concern about the limits of human centered design. Designers are often asked to focus on the needs of a customer or the people who will use a building, product, or service. But the effects do not stop with those people.

Making a product can require water, energy, and raw materials. Building on a site can affect soil, trees, and animal habitats. Those consequences belong in the research and decisions, alongside human needs. Life centered design asks us to consider the natural world from the beginning.

A 2018 scientific study of Earth's biomass estimated that humans account for approximately 0.01% of all biomass measured by carbon mass. This is a measure of mass, not a count of species. It helps put the ecological impact of human activity into perspective.

Biodesign also offers ways to work directly with biology, including research involving fungi, algae, and bacteria. Other work examines biodiversity, materials, habitats, and what happens to products after they are no longer needed. Read more about biodesign at UC Davis.

Where to use the research

Questions for education and industry

The model can help students, teachers, clients, and designers look more closely at the consequences of their work. It can be used when teaching, planning, selecting materials, working with communities, and reviewing what happens after something is made.

Teaching design

Include local culture, ecology, manufacturing, research, and responsibility in studio assignments and classroom discussions.

Writing the brief

Establish who the work is for, who else may be affected, and what needs to be understood before choosing a direction.

Choosing how to make it

Consider materials, suppliers, labor, waste, and the knowledge required to produce the work.

Checking the results

Examine accessibility, repair, local response, costs of upkeep, and environmental effects over time.
