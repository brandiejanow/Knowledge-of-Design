KoD / Ongoing research program

Design Ecosystems

Design operates through relationships between communities, cultures, institutions, materials, the environment, and the people who make decisions.

These relationships matter before a graphic design brief, an interior renovation, a product launch, a public service, or a major building project. The research examines who participates, how local knowledge and cultural practices are respected, what happens to materials and habitats, and how decisions affect people and other forms of life over time.

Lead Researcher and Author: Brandie JanowArchitectural advisor: Nathan Saint Clare

Research model

The layers of a design ecosystem

The design industry includes education, research, commissions, policy, production, distribution, use, and continuing care. Six areas help us examine decisions across these activities. They apply throughout design, although a graphic designer, fashion practitioner, interior designer, or architect may encounter them differently.

Across design disciplines

Before the brief

A visual identity can influence how a community is represented through imagery, language, printing, and access. An interior affects the people who use and maintain a space, the workers who build it, and the materials it requires. A building in another country demands careful attention to local cultures, public life, habitats, and those who will use and maintain it.

The scale changes, but the questions remain: What is already there? Whose knowledge is needed? Which assumptions need questioning before the work begins?

Research questions

Culture, community, and decision making

Every design brief enters a cultural and environmental context that existed before the work began. The research examines how designers learn from communities, respect local practices, understand their surroundings, and account for who or what may be affected by decisions.

Who defines the need?

Have communities been consulted before a brief is agreed? Whose priorities and assumptions determine the work?

What does the culture tell us?

How do local customs, languages, histories, shared places, and lived experiences inform decisions, especially when designers work outside their own communities?

Whose knowledge counts?

How do local expertise, research, craft, technical knowledge, and the experience of those affected enter the process and receive credit?

What happens beyond the intended user?

How will choices affect workers, people outside the target audience, plants, animals, habitats, and the ability to maintain or change the work?

Field observations

Learning from design practice

During a May 2025 visit to the Design Museum in London, exhibitions provided examples of how designers, makers, users, and institutions describe work, production knowledge, material choices, and responsibility. These observations inform the research, but they are not a survey of the design industry.

People and authorship

Design involves many contributors.

Exhibition material foregrounded makers, collaborators, producers, and users alongside those credited with directing a project.

Production and materials

Making involves specialist knowledge.

Fabrication records, textile processes, and material displays illustrated decisions and skills involved in bringing work into use.

Public knowledge

Institutions preserve the process.

Exhibition interpretation and educational programs demonstrated ways to record, examine, and share design knowledge.

Life-centric research

Design for more than people

One reason for establishing KoD was a concern about the limits of human-centered design. Design decisions influence more than the intended users. They affect land, water, plants, animals, habitats, and the conditions other species need to live. Life-centered research asks designers to consider these effects at the start, not only after work is finished.

In a 2018 scientific estimate, humans accounted for approximately 0.01% of Earth's total biomass measured by carbon mass, despite the extensive ecological effects of human activity. That percentage measures biomass, not numbers of species or individuals. Bar-On, Phillips and Milo, PNAS (2018).

Biodesign is one approach that connects design and biology, including work with organisms such as fungi, algae, and bacteria. Other approaches examine biodiversity, habitat health, material choices, and how places and products affect life beyond their intended human users. UC Davis, What Is Biodesign? (2021).

Applying the research

Using the model across design

The six areas offer a way to examine existing design practices, write more informed briefs, teach design methods, and review decisions. They apply across the industry, not just within one type of project.

Education and research

Study how designers learn about culture, local knowledge, ecology, technical work, and consequences of decisions.

Before commissioning

Ask whose needs define a brief, which communities should participate, and what environmental responsibilities come with the work.

Across design practice

Use the six areas to compare graphic design, fashion, products, interiors, services, architecture, and other disciplines.

After delivery

Examine use, repair, unintended effects, accessibility, habitat impacts, and responsibility when conditions change.
